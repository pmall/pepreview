Generative Methods for De Novo Binder Design: Architectural Trade-offs and Experimental Reality

Source: draft/manuscript.md · Figures and tables omitted · Click any paragraph (P01) or sentence (P01.1) badge to copy its code.

Abstract

Reviewed
6 sentences · ~148 words
Advances in protein structure prediction and biomolecular co-folding have enabled computational de novo binder design against specific protein targets.
Generative models conditioned on target structures now narrow experimental testing from massive empirical libraries to tens of candidates per target across peptides, minibinders, and antibodies.
This review examines how multi-chain structure predictors are turned into binder-design workflows, focusing on two core mechanistic trade-offs: how evolutionary information enters the network and how atomic coordinates are generated.
In particular, we discuss how replacing multiple sequence alignments with protein language models enables native single-sequence optimization, and how coordinate-generation choices balance speed, differentiability, and chemical generality.
We also examine why structural confidence filters remain an imperfect guide to candidate selection and why experimental differences prevent treating published wet-lab hit rates as direct comparisons.
Finally, we outline the main technical and translational bottlenecks that still separate in vitro binding hits from developable drug leads.

Introduction

Reviewed
4 sentences · ~113 words
Artificial intelligence transformed structural biology by bringing protein structure prediction to near-experimental accuracy.
AlphaFold2 marked this turning point in the CASP14 assessment, showing that coevolutionary signals in multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) can recover single-chain folds to a median backbone accuracy of 0.96 Å r.m.s.d.95, compared with 2.8 Å for the next best method [@alphafold2].
AlphaFold3 subsequently generalized this framework to complexes containing proteins, nucleic acids, small-molecule ligands, and ions, establishing biomolecular co-folding as a general modeling paradigm [@alphafold3].
With accurate complex prediction in hand, the field has moved quickly to the inverse challenge: repurposing these neural architectures not merely to fold known proteins, but to generate de novo binders against specific protein targets.
Reviewed
5 sentences · ~138 words
Moving from complex prediction to binder generation draws on the same core architecture.
Supplying the sequences of all interacting chains asks the network to predict the three-dimensional complex, whereas leaving the binder chain masked or initialized as noise asks the same model to generate a complementary partner [@rfdiffusion; @boltzgen].
What differs sharply in practice is search and validation.
During training and benchmarking, a structure predictor evaluates a fixed natural sequence whose predicted geometry can be scored directly against a held-out crystal or cryo-electron microscopy structure in the Protein Data Bank (PDB).
By contrast, de novo design must search a combinatorial space of 20L sequences for a chain of length L, lacks natural evolutionary alignments for the invented binder, and has no pre-existing structural reference, so binding can only be confirmed by synthesizing and assaying candidates in a wet laboratory.
Reviewed
4 sentences · ~116 words
Many therapeutically relevant protein targets present broad, flat surfaces that lack the deep pockets required by small-molecule drugs.
Engaging these surfaces calls for peptides, compact de novo proteins (minibinders), or antibody formats such as nanobodies (VHHs), single-chain variable fragments (scFvs), and monoclonal antibodies (mAbs).
Conventional discovery obtains such binders by screening vast random display libraries or immunizing animals, a process that must be repeated for each new target and offers limited control over the binding site.
Generative and filtering pipelines instead propose candidate sequences directly against a chosen target structure, narrowing experimental synthesis budgets from thousands or millions of variants in display libraries and early computational campaigns [@chai2; @rfantibody] down to tens of tested designs per target [@chai2; @rfpeptides; @boltzgen; @protenix_v2].
Reviewed
3 sentences · ~98 words
Written from a drug-discovery and protein-engineering perspective, this review examines these protein- and peptide-design methods, emphasizing underlying mechanisms and architectural trade-offs rather than ranking models.
We first examine how structure-prediction trunks and coordinate-generation modules operate, then trace how those components give rise to distinct binder-design workflows, evaluate computational filtering limits alongside wet-lab campaign evidence, and conclude with the translational and technical bottlenecks separating in vitro binding hits from viable drug leads.
Small-molecule drug design, which operates on variable chemical graphs governed by valence rules, requires separate architectures and benchmarks and therefore lies outside the scope of this review.

1. Co-folding Architectures and Mechanistic Trade-offs

The two-stage co-folding pipeline

Reviewed
7 sentences · ~208 words
Modern protein structure prediction rests on the principle that correlated mutations between positions in an MSA reflect spatial proximity in the folded three-dimensional structure [@alphafold2].
Whereas early RoseTTAFold architectures propagated information through parallel one-dimensional sequence, two-dimensional pairwise distance, and three-dimensional coordinate tracks from input to output, allowing spatial motifs and target geometry to be supplied directly as coordinates [@rosettafold; @rosettafold2], most contemporary co-folding models adopt a two-stage pipeline (Fig 1).
In the first stage, a deep sequence-to-pairwise reasoning trunk transforms sequence and evolutionary inputs into single-residue and pairwise residue representations.
Examples include the Evoformer in AlphaFold2 [@alphafold2], the simplified Pairformer shared by AlphaFold3, Boltz, Chai-1, Protenix, and RoseTTAFold3 [@alphafold3; @boltz1; @chai1; @protenix_v1; @rosettafold3], and the recurrent folding trunk in ESMFold and ESMFold2 [@esm2; @esmc].
A linear projection on the pairwise representation predicts a distogram, giving a probability distribution over binned physical three-dimensional distances (typically 2 to 22 Å in 64 bins) for every residue pair.
Crucially for design, the entire trunk is differentiable and executes in a single forward pass for a given protein complex [@boltz1], allowing the distogram to serve both as a training loss and as a smooth surrogate objective during sequence optimization [@alphafold2; @boltzdesign1; @esmc].
In the second stage, a dedicated coordinate module converts these learned trunk representations into explicit three-dimensional atomic positions.

Evolutionary inputs: multiple sequence alignments versus protein language models

Reviewed
5 sentences · ~108 words
How evolutionary information enters the trunk creates one of the deepest mechanistic divides in binder design.
Standard MSA-based co-folders, including AlphaFold2, AlphaFold3, RoseTTAFold3, Boltz, and Protenix, retrieve homologous sequences from large sequence databases at inference time [@alphafold2; @alphafold3; @rosettafold3; @boltz1; @protenix_v1].
Database retrieval is inherently discrete and non-differentiable, and it is undefined for a synthetic binder sequence that has no natural homologs.
Consequently, whenever an MSA-trained model evaluates or optimizes a de novo binder chain, that chain must run in single-sequence mode (without an MSA), even if the target protein carries a deep alignment.
Both the AlphaFold2 and AlphaFold3 publications document that shallow-MSA and single-sequence inputs represent their weakest accuracy regime [@alphafold2; @alphafold3].
Reviewed
6 sentences · ~175 words
Replacing database lookups with a protein language model embeds evolutionary statistics directly into network weights learned from billions of natural sequences.
ESM-2 and ESMFold established that single-sequence structure prediction without MSAs or templates is feasible, initially trading lower accuracy than AlphaFold2 for substantially faster inference [@esm2].
Recent scaling of this paradigm has closed much of that gap for complex interfaces.
ESMC is a protein language model trained on roughly 2.8 billion sequences, whose frozen 6-billion-parameter representations drive ESMFold2 through 48 recurrent pair-state folding layers (24 in ESMFold2-Fast) and a sliding-window atom-attention diffusion transformer [@esmc].
Without MSAs, ESMFold2 matches MSA-enabled AlphaFold3 on antibody–antigen structure prediction, a parity confirmed by independent AtlasFold benchmarks [@esmc; @atlasfold], while retaining an optional detachable MSA encoder for unusually high-perplexity sequences [@esmc].
For binder design, the architectural payoff is direct: because a protein language model trunk expects single sequences natively, gradients from the folding trunk and distogram can propagate all the way back through the language model into the input sequence representation without pushing the designed chain outside the model's training distribution [@esmc].
Reviewed
4 sentences · ~77 words
Hybrid architectures bridge these two regimes.
Chai-1 incorporates both an MSA track and a protein language model track that can operate independently or jointly, alongside optional experimental contact or pocket restraints [@chai1].
On antibody–protein interfaces, where natural cross-chain coevolution is weak, single-sequence Chai-1 matches MSA-enabled Chai-1 and outperforms MSA-driven AlphaFold-Multimer v2.3 [@chai1].
Together, these results highlight why single-sequence language-model trunks are particularly well suited to designed peptides, minibinders, and antibody loops whose sequences lack paired natural alignments.

Coordinate-generation strategies and physical stereochemistry

Reviewed
4 sentences · ~105 words
The second major architectural choice governs how trunk representations become atomic coordinates, trading off inference speed, chemical generality, and end-to-end differentiability.
Single-pass coordinate regression, introduced in AlphaFold2's Invariant Point Attention (IPA) structure module and adopted in ESMFold, updates rigid residue frames directly in a single forward pass [@alphafold2; @esm2].
Because coordinates are regressed in one shallow, deterministic pass rather than over hundreds of iterative steps, gradients from three-dimensional coordinate and confidence losses propagate cleanly all the way back to the input sequence [@alphafold2; @bindcraft].
However, frame-based IPA modules are tailored to canonical amino-acid backbones and do not naturally generalize to arbitrary non-protein atoms or unusual covalent chemistries.
Reviewed
7 sentences · ~188 words
To unify proteins, nucleic acids, small molecules, ions, and modified or cyclized peptides within one coordinate head, AlphaFold3 replaced IPA with an iterative all-atom diffusion module that transforms Gaussian noise into atomic coordinates along a stochastic differential equation trajectory [@alphafold3].
Subsequent all-atom co-folders, including Boltz, Chai, Protenix, RoseTTAFold3, and ESMFold2, adopted variants of this diffusion formalism [@boltz1; @chai1; @protenix_v1; @rosettafold3; @esmc].
Unlike single-pass regression, stochastic diffusion samples from a distribution of plausible structures instead of outputting one deterministic pose.
In practice, co-folders generate a pool of candidate structures for a given complex by running multiple random seeds of the overall network and drawing a batch of diffusion samples per trunk pass (five model seeds of five diffusion samples each in standard AlphaFold3 inference), then use an internal confidence head to select one top-ranked prediction [@alphafold3].
Diffusion brings broad chemical generality at two costs relevant to design.
First, iterative denoising is slow, with protein diffusion generators often requiring hundreds to roughly 1,000 forward passes per sample [@frameflow].
Second, unrolling hundreds of stochastic denoising steps to compute sequence gradients is computationally impractical, breaking the simple end-to-end backpropagation path enjoyed by IPA regression models [@boltzdesign1].
Reviewed
3 sentences · ~77 words
Flow matching addresses the sampling bottleneck by formulating coordinate generation as an ordinary differential equation (ODE) with straighter transport trajectories requiring far fewer integration steps.
On unconditional protein monomers, FrameFlow recast frame diffusion as flow matching and reported twofold higher designability at five times fewer timesteps [@frameflow].
Target-conditioned flow-matching generators, including PPIFlow, OriginFlow, AtomFlow, and D-Flow, extend these faster trajectories to protein–protein interfaces [@ppiflow; @originflow; @atomflow; @dflow], and few-step ODE decoders can additionally restore end-to-end differentiability through all-atom coordinates [@pxdesign].
Reviewed
5 sentences · ~108 words
Predicting free three-dimensional positions for every atom in all-atom diffusion models also introduces physical-validity errors that rigid amino-acid frames largely avoided.
Because bond geometry and handedness are learned from data rather than fixed by residue templates, AlphaFold3 still reported occasional steric clashes and a 4.4% chirality violation rate on the PoseBusters benchmark despite ranking penalties [@alphafold3].
Two strategies address this limitation.
Boltz-1x applies explicit steering penalties during denoising to suppress clashes and inverted stereocenters [@boltz1].
RoseTTAFold3 instead encodes stereochemistry during training to avoid perturbing inference trajectories, achieving 1.74 Å mean backbone RMSD on mixed L/D peptides compared with 1.88 Å for AlphaFold3 and 2.24 Å for Boltz-1x [@rosettafold3].

2. Design Workflows: Turning Predictors into Binder Generators

Reviewed
5 sentences · ~131 words
Because structure predictors evaluate a fixed sequence without searching sequence space, practical binder-design systems repurpose them through three primary workflows (Fig 1B).
The first generates a three-dimensional binder geometry (either a backbone alone or an all-atom structure including side chains) against the target, assigns a compatible sequence with a separate inverse-folding model, and refolds the complex to filter candidates.
The second trains a single all-atom diffusion network to generate atomic coordinates and amino-acid identities simultaneously, eliminating the inverse-folding handoff before refolding-based filtering.
The third keeps a pre-trained structure predictor fixed and optimizes the binder sequence by gradient descent until it predicts a high-confidence complex.
Table 1 summarizes representative open and commercial systems from these three paradigms, highlighting how trunk inputs, coordinate formalisms, supported modalities, and methodological openness vary across the field.

Structure generation with inverse folding

Reviewed
7 sentences · ~179 words
The most widely adopted binder-design pipeline decouples geometry generation from sequence assignment, operating without backpropagation during design.
RFdiffusion popularized this generate-and-filter workflow by fine-tuning RoseTTAFold predictor weights to denoise three-dimensional residue frames against a fixed target protein, relying on RoseTTAFold's coordinate track to condition directly on target geometry [@rfdiffusion].
RFdiffusion outputs only a sequence-free backbone, so an inverse-folding network, most commonly ProteinMPNN, next assigns compatible amino-acid sequences to the generated geometry [@proteinmpnn].
Candidates are then refolded in complex with the target by a structure predictor such as AlphaFold2 (adapted for complexes via chain-break offsets or AlphaFold-Multimer) and triaged on confidence and self-consistency thresholds, exemplified by AlphaProteo's widely referenced cutoffs of interchain PAE below 10 Å, binder-aligned RMSD below 1 Å, and pLDDT above 80 [@rfdiffusion; @alphaproteo].
Two caveats apply throughout this workflow.
First, as the ProteinMPNN authors noted, high native sequence recovery on offline fixed-backbone benchmarks does not necessarily predict whether a designed sequence will fold accurately [@proteinmpnn].
Second, the "in silico success" rates frequently quoted for backbone generators measure self-consistency with a refolding predictor, not experimental target engagement [@rfdiffusion].
Reviewed
6 sentences · ~174 words
Targeted architectural modifications extend this decoupled paradigm to new modalities and scales.
RFdiffusion3 moved away from fine-tuning a RoseTTAFold predictor altogether, instead pre-training a compact 168-million-parameter all-atom transformer U-Net modeled on AlphaFold3's diffusion module directly on PDB and AlphaFold2 distillation structures [@rfdiffusion3].
By trimming its Pairformer trunk from 48 to 2 layers and sampling in 100 steps, RFdiffusion3 runs roughly an order of magnitude faster than earlier releases, using LigandMPNN in place of ProteinMPNN whenever non-protein atoms such as ligands, nucleic acids, or metal ions are present [@rfdiffusion3; @ligandmpnn].
For specialized topologies such as head-to-tail macrocycles, RFpeptides adds a cyclic relative positional encoding to RFdiffusion and RoseTTAFold2 [@rfpeptides].
After inverse folding and cyclic AlphaFold2 (AfCycDesign) filtering, it produced crystal-structure-verified binders against four targets from fewer than 20 synthesized peptides each [@rfpeptides; @afcycdesign].
Related variants in Table 1 explore autoregressive diffusion for inverse folding in AlphaDesign [@alphadesign], fine-tune diffusion for antibody loops in RFantibody [@rfantibody], replace frame representations with Cartesian diffusion transformers in PXDesign-d [@pxdesign], or accelerate sampling through ODE flow matching in PPIFlow and AtomFlow [@ppiflow; @atomflow].

Unified prediction-and-design all-atom models

Reviewed
5 sentences · ~142 words
A second workflow eliminates the handoff between separate geometry generators and inverse-folding models by training a single all-atom diffusion network jointly for structure prediction and de novo sequence–structure generation.
BoltzGen exemplifies this unified approach under a fully reproducible MIT release providing model weights, training code, and training datasets [@boltzgen].
Conditioned on a target, BoltzGen simultaneously generates atomic coordinates and amino-acid identities for masked binder chains, covering peptides, minibinders, and nanobodies (full antibodies were excluded from training) [@boltzgen].
It matches the structure-prediction accuracy of Boltz-2 on the same benchmark test set, yet the design pipeline still relies on that predictor to refold and filter generated candidates [@boltzgen].
While commercial systems such as Chai-2 and Latent-X may use related co-design strategies internally, their generative architectures remain undisclosed [@chai2; @latentx1; @latentx2], leaving BoltzGen (and its API-only follow-up BoltzProt-1 [@boltzprot1]) as the clearest open representative of this paradigm.
Reviewed
3 sentences · ~85 words
Joint all-atom training also exposes how generative diffusion models memorize biases in the Protein Data Bank.
The BoltzGen authors transparently documented that at designed binder lengths between 73 and 76 residues, structural diversity collapses as the model overwhelmingly samples ubiquitin, an artifact traced to the presence of more than 900 ubiquitin entries in the PDB [@boltzgen].
More broadly, the authors caution that even unified all-atom design is neither zero-shot nor plug-and-play in practice, recommending manual structural inspection and iterative reruns over unexamined automated selection [@boltzgen].

Gradient-based sequence optimization

Reviewed
5 sentences · ~147 words
The third workflow turns a pre-trained structure predictor into a generator via gradient descent, often termed hallucination or model inversion.
Because discrete amino-acid identities cannot be differentiated directly, the binder sequence is relaxed into a continuous probability distribution over the 20 amino acids at each of L positions (an L × 20 matrix of amino-acid weights).
At each optimization step, this soft sequence is passed alongside the target through the differentiable predictor to compute a design loss favoring confident folding and tight interchain contacts.
Gradients of that loss flow backward through the network to update the underlying sequence weights, nudging each position toward amino acids that improve the predicted complex.
To produce a real protein with a single amino acid at every position, a temperature-annealing schedule gradually sharpens these distributions over iterations until each position commits to a discrete one-hot residue, a workflow popularized by ColabDesign [@colabdesign].
Reviewed
6 sentences · ~194 words
When the predictor uses a single-pass IPA regression module, gradients propagate cleanly from predicted three-dimensional coordinates and confidence heads back to the input sequence [@alphafold2].
BindCraft leverages this full-network differentiability in AlphaFold-Multimer, cycling randomly among all five trained parameter sets between iterations to curb overfitting [@bindcraft].
It sharpens the binder sequence over four stages, progressing from continuous amino-acid weights to discrete residues (passing discrete sequences forward and continuous gradients backward) and final single-mutation refinement, after which a soluble ProteinMPNN variant redesigns non-interface residues to improve stability and solubility [@bindcraft].
A notable mechanistic feature of BindCraft is that it repredicts the target structure at every optimization step, permitting induced-fit target rearrangements ranging from 0.5 to 5.5 Å Cα RMSD [@bindcraft].
For cyclic peptides, AfCycDesign applies a cyclic relative positional offset to AlphaFold2's peptide chain while keeping the target linear, yielding eight monomer crystal structures within 1.0 Å of design, though its MDM2 and Keap1 binders relied on motif grafting rather than direct de novo hallucination [@afcycdesign].
However, both BindCraft and AfCycDesign rely on MSA-trained AlphaFold2 models even though the invented binder chain has no evolutionary alignment, forcing optimization into the predictor's weakest, MSA-free regime [@alphafold2; @bindcraft].
Reviewed
6 sentences · ~184 words
Adapting gradient optimization to diffusion-based co-folders requires circumventing the cost of differentiating through hundreds of denoising steps [@boltzdesign1].
Two solutions have proved effective.
The first computes gradients exclusively from the differentiable trunk's distogram and confidence heads while placing a stop-gradient on the diffusion module, preventing gradients from flowing back through the iterative denoising steps.
BoltzDesign1 applies this strategy to Boltz-1's 64-bin Pairformer distogram, optimizing the underlying distance distribution from which diffusion samples; on 212 BindCraft binders, Pairformer contacts matched diffusion-sampled contacts on 76% of designs, but nucleic-acid targets required confidence-head losses to maintain correlation [@boltzdesign1].
ESMFold2 pairs this distogram-gradient strategy with its native single-sequence language-model trunk, running single-stage continuous optimization directly through both the 6-billion-parameter ESMC language model and two ESMFold2 trunk replicates before invoking the diffusion and confidence modules only in final steps to score ipTM [@esmc].
The second solution preserves coordinate-level backpropagation by replacing the iterative diffusion sampler with a few-step deterministic decoder: built on the open AlphaFold3-style predictor Protenix, PXDesign-h substitutes a differentiable two-step ODE sampler for Protenix's 200-step diffusion module and optimizes against an ensemble of five resampled models [@pxdesign].
Reviewed
7 sentences · ~148 words
Optimizing a sequence solely for high predicted confidence can produce unnatural amino-acid compositions that exploit a single predictor's blind spots rather than genuinely folding and binding.
To prevent such adversarial designs, recent gradient methods add language-model priors or restrict search to discrete mutations.
Native language-model trunks such as ESMC guide designed sequences toward natural protein statistics intrinsically during backpropagation [@esmc].
For MSA-based predictors, external language models can provide a similar guardrail: Germinal merges live gradients from an antibody language model (IgLM) with AlphaFold-Multimer confidence gradients [@germinal], whereas mBER incorporates fixed sequence preferences from ESM-2 [@mber].
Taking a complementary route, RFOptimization avoids continuous sequence relaxation altogether [@rfoptimization].
It keeps sequences strictly discrete, using RoseTTAFold3 gradients only to propose single-point mutations that are accepted or rejected iteratively.
To curb overfitting to any single predictor, it interleaves Boltz refolding with LigandMPNN resampling while holding out AlphaFold3 as an independent check [@rfoptimization].

3. Candidate Selection and Experimental Evidence

Candidate selection and filtering limits

Reviewed
6 sentences · ~157 words
Evaluating a structure predictor against an experimentally solved reference uses external geometric metrics, including DockQ interface accuracy [@dockq] and atomic RMSD [@kabsch].
As de novo binders lack an experimental structure, every design workflow relies on internal self-consistency checks and model-estimated confidence scores to select the small subset of candidates ordered for wet-lab testing.
Standard filters combine self-consistency RMSD between generated and refolded coordinates, per-residue confidence (pLDDT) [@alphafold2], interface predicted TM-score (ipTM), and interchain predicted aligned error (iPAE) [@alphafold_multimer].
More recent pipelines also adopt ipSAE, which restricts ipTM to interchain residue pairs below a fixed PAE threshold so that disordered tails or arbitrary chain trimming do not dilute interface scores [@ipsae].
Yet structure-prediction confidence heads are trained on experimentally solved bound complexes to estimate coordinate error, not on nonbinders or binding thermodynamics, so high structural confidence does not guarantee wet-lab binding on unseen designs.
Three lines of evidence show why structural confidence remains an imperfect guide to candidate selection.
Reviewed
4 sentences · ~160 words
As noted in Section 1, co-folding predictors generate a pool of candidate structures for each complex and rely on internal confidence scores to select a top prediction, but even on known complexes those confidence heads routinely fail to rank the most accurate binding pose first.
Across seven predictors evaluated on FoldBench, a benchmark of experimentally solved complexes [@foldbench], comparing each model's confidence-ranked top prediction against the best structure actually generated in the same run revealed a consistent ranking loss [@atlasfold].
Specifically, confidence ranking missed an acceptable interface (DockQ > 0.23) already present among the generated samples on 11 to 21% of antibody–antigen complexes (4.5% for Boltz-1) and 3 to 8% of general protein–protein interfaces [@atlasfold].
The FoldBench study itself noted a closely related failure mode for antibodies: a predictor can model the key CDR-H3 binding loop accurately and still dock the antibody at the wrong site, and per-sample confidence scores do not reliably separate near-native poses from misdocked decoys [@foldbench].
Reviewed
5 sentences · ~137 words
While confidence filters raise hit rates in large candidate pools [@mber], their accuracy in predicting wet-lab binding fluctuates sharply from one target to another.
Overath et al. illustrated this variability by re-scoring 3,766 experimentally tested designs against 15 targets from six published campaigns through a uniform pipeline [@overath_meta].
AlphaFold3 ipSAE emerged as the strongest single predictor of binding overall, though its average precision spanned 0.1 to 1.0 depending on the target (bearing in mind that the pooled set retains heterogeneous source assays and earlier-generation generators) [@overath_meta].
In the Germinal antibody campaign, many experimental nonbinders cleared the exact same ipSAE threshold as validated binders [@germinal].
Evaluating refolding filters on a shared experimental benchmark, PXDesign found that Protenix and AlphaFold2 selected largely different subsets of true binders and that no single cutoff maximized accuracy on all targets simultaneously [@pxdesign].
Reviewed
4 sentences · ~98 words
Replacing structure-prediction confidence with classifiers trained directly on wet-lab binding outcomes can also substantially improve hit rates on identical candidate pools.
In a controlled comparison on 10 low-homology targets (150 designs total), BoltzProt-1 kept the BoltzGen generator and 15-design-per-target testing budget fixed and replaced BoltzGen's confidence scores with BoltzPPI, a classifier trained on experimental binding data [@boltzprot1].
This change alone more than doubled confirmed binders from 3.3% (5/150, yielding hits on 2 of 10 targets) to 8.0% (12/150, yielding hits on 4 of 10 targets) [@boltzprot1].
Candidate ranking therefore shapes experimental yield independently of the underlying generative architecture.

Reading wet-lab campaigns critically

Reviewed
3 sentences · ~83 words
Over the past three years, experimental campaigns spanning compact minibinders, nanobodies, antibodies, and cyclic peptides have demonstrated that computational de novo design can routinely yield nanomolar and occasionally picomolar binders from modest synthesis budgets.
Table 2 compiles representative campaigns grouped by modality alongside their target scope, testing budgets, binding criteria, and experimental caveats.
Because no two de novo campaigns share a common target panel, assay format, or positivity threshold, headline hit rates should be read as context-dependent demonstrations rather than direct head-to-head rankings.
Reviewed
6 sentences · ~222 words
Three experimental factors explain much of the apparent spread in published hit rates and affinities.
Hit definitions and assay rigor vary widely: some studies call binders from a single permissive screen, such as a qualitative surface plasmon resonance (SPR) signal without a numeric cutoff [@bindcraft; @rfpeptides; @latentx1] or a small single-point biolayer interferometry (BLI) shift of 0.05 to 0.1 nm above background [@protenix_v2; @pxdesign; @chai2], whereas others verify candidates in a second, independent assay that eliminates many initial hits.
For example, BoltzProt-1 separates initial screening hits (comparable to what earlier campaigns typically report as binders) from confirmed binders verified by reversing sensor immobilization orientation and independent vendor re-testing [@boltzprot1], and Germinal similarly confirmed Fab hits by both SPR and BLI [@germinal].
Multivalent avidity also inflates apparent affinities when binders are tested as dimeric Fc fusions or under dense sensor-immobilization setups that allow simultaneous multi-site engagement, a caveat flagged on headline picomolar and sub-nanomolar hits in Protenix-v2, BindCraft, and Chai-2 [@protenix_v2; @bindcraft; @chai2].
Finally, excluding constructs that fail chemical synthesis or protein expression shrinks the hit-rate denominator and inflates reported success rates.
In practice, cyclic-peptide campaigns such as RFpeptides and Latent-X1 often assayed only about half of the sequences selected for synthesis [@rfpeptides; @latentx1], and PXDesign showed on PD-L1 that omitting six poorly expressing designs raised its reported hit rate from 47.1% of ordered constructs to 72.7% of tested constructs [@pxdesign].
Reviewed
9 sentences · ~283 words
Target and epitope selection biases are equally consequential.
Most published campaigns test a handful of targets and epitopes selected by the authors, yet binding difficulty varies sharply between sites on a single protein: two epitopes on the same antigen (AMBP) in Protenix-v2 yielded 4% and 48% hit rates [@protenix_v2].
The scale of this selection effect was quantified by mBER, which designed more than 1.15 million unique VHHs against 436 human cell-surface targets (145 assayed by phage display without affinity measurement) targeting randomly sampled exposed surface hotspots [@mber].
Across uncurated hotspots, mBER's median per-design hit rate was roughly 0.4%, two orders of magnitude below curated small-panel headlines, while its best individual hotspots reached 7.5% overall and 38% (8/21) within an ipTM-filtered bin [@mber].
Even when identical molecules are re-tested in different laboratories, assay conditions shift measured performance.
When AlphaProteo resynthesized RFdiffusion's full published panels (including nonbinders) for yeast display at 10- to 20-fold lower target concentrations, hit rates fell from 33% to 16% on IL-7Rα and from 6% to 0% on TrkA [@alphaproteo; @rfdiffusion].
Similarly, when Latent-X1 resynthesized RFpeptides' two best macrocycles and re-tested them in its own SPR assay, the exact same peptides measured 4.4- to 5-fold weaker than originally published, shifting from 1.90 to 8.38 µM on MDM2 and from 2.0 to 10.0 µM on MCL-1 [@latentx1; @rfpeptides].
Given how strongly assay choices, target curation, and post-hoc filtering shape reported hit rates, wet-lab claims from closed commercial systems cannot be independently verified or reproduced when their architectures, weights, and filtering pipelines remain undisclosed [@bender_perspective].
This limitation applies to AlphaProteo [@alphaproteo], Chai-2 [@chai2], Latent-X1 and Latent-X2 [@latentx1; @latentx2], API-only BoltzProt-1 [@boltzprot1], and unpublished models such as IsoDDE, which is known from third-party scaling plots alone [@opendde].

4. From In Vitro Binding Hits to Drug Leads

The translational gap

Reviewed
4 sentences · ~131 words
Nearly every de novo binder-design study frames its work around therapeutic discovery, from accelerating biologic drug development to engineering species cross-reactivity or blocking clinically relevant interactions [@boltzgen; @chai2; @bindcraft; @latentx2; @mber].
Reviewing a decade of artificial intelligence in pharmaceutical research, Bender et al. draw a useful distinction between model validation and process validation [@bender_perspective].
Model validation measures performance on an isolated task, such as predicting accurate complex structures or identifying nanomolar in vitro hits, whereas process validation asks the harder translational question of whether those outputs actually improve downstream drug-discovery decisions and reduce clinical failure [@bender_perspective].
Public databases already contain more than 106 bioactive small molecules compared with roughly 103 marketed drugs, a reminder that confirming target binding in a biophysical assay is only the first step toward a viable peptide or protein drug [@bender_perspective].
Reviewed
6 sentences · ~158 words
Clinical viability simultaneously demands high expression, solubility, thermal stability, low self-association, minimal immunogenicity, suitable pharmacokinetics, and the intended functional modulation beyond passive binding [@bender_perspective; @latentx2].
The blinded AIntibody challenge offered a stark empirical illustration of this trade-off: on its out-of-library engineering task, 30.4% of computational designs failed to bind, and the highest-affinity binder in the entire competition (2.9 pM) failed to elute from a hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC) developability column [@aintibody].
Despite these requirements, nearly all open generative frameworks still optimize and filter exclusively for predicted binding geometry, leaving developability and immunogenicity to downstream screening.
Early results from closed platforms suggest that joint optimization of binding and drug-like properties is nevertheless achievable.
For example, Latent-X2 reports de novo macrocycles and antibodies whose developability and low-immunogenicity profiles match or exceed approved therapeutics, including binders on 9 of 18 antibody targets down to 26.2 pM [@latentx2].
Yet its undisclosed architecture prevents independent analysis of how those competing properties are balanced [@latentx2].

Quantitative protein–protein affinity prediction

Reviewed
6 sentences · ~167 words
Even once a campaign identifies an initial binder, optimizing its potency still lacks reliable computational guidance, as predicting quantitative binding affinity (KD) for protein–protein complexes is still unsolved.
Current co-folding confidence scores behave mainly as binary indicators of whether a complex forms rather than calibrated free-energy estimators, so they cannot distinguish weak micromolar hits from nanomolar leads or guide affinity-maturing mutations.
BindCraft explicitly notes that AlphaFold2 ipTM separates binders from nonbinders without predicting how tightly they bind [@bindcraft].
The same bottleneck appeared across computational methods in the blinded AIntibody challenge, where only 1 of 26 participating groups outperformed random selection when ranking closely related antibody clones (sharing the same HCDR3 loop) by binding strength [@aintibody].
Dedicated affinity-prediction heads added onto co-folding models, such as Boltz-2's binding-affinity head, have so far succeeded on small-molecule free-energy benchmarks, but they are trained exclusively on single-protein ligand assays and do not apply to multimeric protein–protein interactions [@boltz2].
Consequently, potency ranking and affinity maturation continue to depend on experimental kinetics after design.

Targets without experimental structures and conformational dynamics

Reviewed
6 sentences · ~177 words
Current design workflows depend heavily on static, experimentally solved target structures and struggle with flexible, uncharacterized, or intrinsically disordered proteins.
Nearly all successful wet-lab campaigns so far have started from an experimental crystal or cryo-electron microscopy structure of the target protein, or from a well-folded predicted domain [@rfdiffusion; @bindcraft; @rfpeptides; @latentx1].
For structurally uncharacterized targets without a solved structure, how reliably binder design works when starting solely from a predicted target model is still largely untested, a caveat Germinal also noted after running designs against AlphaFold3-predicted targets [@germinal].
Flexible loops and intrinsically disordered regions pose an even deeper challenge because they lack a single fixed conformation in solution, whereas PDB-trained co-folders produce static snapshots and can hallucinate artificial secondary structure in disordered stretches unless mitigated during training [@alphafold3].
Recent campaigns have begun targeting flexible extracellular loops on GPCRs [@protenix_v2] and probing disordered proteins such as NPM1, where BoltzGen observed live-cell nucleolar co-localization for 1 of 5 designed peptides without yet confirming direct binding to the disordered region [@boltzgen].
Overall, reliable design against dynamic or structurally uncharacterized proteins remains an open problem.

Blinded prospective benchmarks for de novo design

Reviewed
6 sentences · ~195 words
Beyond these technical challenges, the field still lacks a standardized, blinded prospective wet-lab benchmark for target-conditioned de novo binder design.
Structure prediction matured rapidly around shared blind assessments such as CASP and unified benchmark suites like FoldBench [@foldbench], where models are scored under common protocols on held-out experimental structures.
De novo binder evaluation is harder to standardize because every proposed sequence must be synthesized, expressed, and assayed physically, leaving the field dependent on disconnected, self-selected campaigns.
Full-campaign independent replication is still rare, with AlphaProteo's re-testing of RFdiffusion's full panels as a notable exception [@alphaproteo; @rfdiffusion], and retrospective meta-analyses such as Overath et al. standardize computational scoring without removing assay heterogeneity among source studies [@overath_meta].
The blinded AIntibody challenge showed that unified wet-lab benchmarking involving 29 organizations is feasible, combining independent affinity kinetics with a five-assay developability panel, but it tested library-conditioned antibody optimization against a single well-characterized antigen (SARS-CoV-2 RBD) rather than target-conditioned de novo design spanning diverse modalities [@aintibody].
Extending blinded, multi-group prospective evaluation to novel targets and uncurated epitopes across peptides, minibinders, and antibodies, with full reporting of ordered constructs, independent binding confirmation, and developability profiling, will be necessary to measure genuine progress cleanly.

Conclusions

Reviewed
7 sentences · ~209 words
Within a few years, biomolecular co-folding models have transformed de novo binder design from massive empirical library screening into targeted computational sequence generation.
For peptides, minibinders, and antibody formats, workflows built on either MSA-based or single-sequence language-model trunks can now yield nanomolar binders from tens of tested candidates on well-characterized targets.
Tracing these systems from their underlying components shows that no single architecture dominates every setting, as each approach carries distinct strengths and limitations in single-sequence differentiability, sampling speed, all-atom chemical generality, and stereochemical fidelity.
At the same time, rapid generative progress has shifted the primary bottleneck away from proposing plausible folds and toward selecting real binders.
Structure-prediction confidence heads estimate coordinate error on solved complexes, not binding thermodynamics, so they still misrank accurate poses, vary widely in accuracy across targets, and can be outperformed by classifiers trained directly on wet-lab outcomes.
Comparing published campaigns further requires looking past headline hit rates to assay rigor, multivalent avidity effects, construct attrition, and target curation, while quantitative protein–protein affinity prediction and flexible or uncharacterized targets remain open technical frontiers.
Turning in vitro binding hits into developable, functionally active drug leads, and testing genuine progress through transparent methods and shared, blinded prospective benchmarks, will define the next stage of computational binder design.
