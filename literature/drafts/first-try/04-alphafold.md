# §1 — AlphaFold, and the open co-folding cluster

Prediction opens this review because AlphaFold is the reference every later
section is defined against: what §2–§8 do is reproduce it openly, vary the
evolutionary input it chose, or answer what its architecture took away. The
reference has two poles — AlphaFold2 for single chains, AlphaFold3 for arbitrary
complexes — and an open reproduction joins each pole here, since neither
reproduction has a lineage of its own to follow.

AlphaFold2 is the pole the rest of the review borrows its standing from, and
what it contributes from here is in three parts. The co-folding anatomy
described in the background — pairwise reasoning refined into a distogram, then
coordinates drawn from it — is AF2's design, and the later sections' co-folders
are variations on it. Its structure stage is the single regression pass that
everything after it replaced, and because the network was trained end to end it
is the one predictor here that a design loop can invert without choosing an
attachment point — the property §5 depends on entirely. And it is also the
standard post-hoc filter across §2–§4 and §6, the review's first model turned
into its most-used critic. Nothing in the review re-argues the pole: against the
CASP14 field it displaced, the coevolution bet is treated as settled, which is
why every later contrast is drawn with AlphaFold2 rather than with anything
before it. The caveat that travels with all of this is AF2's own: it is weakest
run single-sequence, and its paper locates the dependence — accuracy drops
substantially once median alignment depth falls below about thirty sequences,
having flattened once depth passes around a hundred — which is exactly the
regime §5 operates it in.

AlphaFold-Multimer is the complex version of the same machine, and the complex
predictor the review actually runs on: the design systems of §5 do not invert
AlphaFold2, they invert this. Its own account starts from the surprise that
simple input modifications — chains bridged by glycine–serine linkers, or a gap
opened in the residue indexing — let the single-chain model predict complexes
tolerably well. What ended the question was training the network natively on
complexes of known stoichiometry. On a benchmark of 17 template-free
heterodimers, 14 targets reach at least medium interface accuracy (DockQ ≥ 0.49)
and 6 reach high accuracy (≥ 0.8), against 9 and 4 for the best pipeline that
docked AlphaFold's chain predictions, the average DockQ given as 0.65 there
against 0.49. On interface pairs extracted from 4,433 recent PDB complexes and
filtered to below 40% template identity — 2,603 scored pairs — 67% of
heteromeric interfaces clear DockQ ≥ 0.23 and 23% clear ≥ 0.8, 25 and 11 points
above the input-adapted baseline. The paper's other export is the ranking metric
its inference selects on, 0.8·ipTM + 0.2·pTM: this is where ipTM and the
interface PAE reduction enter the literature, the terms the standard success
criterion and half the review's numbers are denominated in. Two caveats travel
with the paper. It is the field's most-used complex predictor and was never
peer-reviewed — a bioRxiv preprint five years on, which is a disclosure gap of a
different kind from §8's. And its discussion flags a limit the review's subject
sits directly on: at release, the authors note anecdotally, AlphaFold-Multimer
generally could not predict antibody binding. A binder design system of 2026
does not inherit that single limitation; what it inherits is the demonstration
that negotiating an interface is a problem of its own, not a corollary of
folding a chain well.

AlphaFold3 carries the review's second structural fact. The network that
generalizes from single chains to complexes of proteins, nucleic acids, small
molecules, ions and modified residues is recognizably AF2's — the trunk's MSA
processing shrunk to four blocks while the 48 blocks of pairwise reasoning are
kept — but the structure stage changed in kind: raw atom coordinates are
generated by diffusion, trained to receive "noised" atomic coordinates and then
predict the true coordinates, replacing the regression pass. That single
replacement is what the rest of the review reacts to. It removed the
differentiability the design loop of §5 needs, and the three answers to that
fact — reattach at the trunk, attach at the confidence heads, shorten the
trajectory — are what §2, §5 and §6 disagree about. Against AlphaFold2 on both
counts — more modalities, at the cost of the property the design half needs — it
is the review's central trade, stated by the same lab in two papers. The ligand
half is measured against classical docking instead. On the PoseBusters
benchmark's 428 protein–ligand structures, released from 2021 onward and
evaluated under a 2019 training cutoff that keeps them out of the model's data,
scoring success as pocket-aligned ligand RMSD under 2 Å, AF3 outperforms Vina
and RoseTTAFold All-Atom while taking no structural input — where the docking
tools it is compared against are handed the solved pocket. The diffusion switch
also brought failures AF2 did not have, and this paper's own limitations section
is where the review's physical-validity thread starts: sampled coordinates can
be chemically impossible, with a 4.4% chirality violation rate still observed
after a chirality penalty was added to the ranking, and clashes that penalizing
reduces without eliminating — the reason PB-valid enters the literature here as
the test for them. Generative training also invents order into disordered
regions, which AF3 counteracts by cross-distillation from AlphaFold-Multimer
v2.3's predictions. Two limits are left standing for sections downstream: the
outputs are static structures — different seeds do not sample an ensemble — and
the MSA-depth dependence is the same as AlphaFold-Multimer's, the dependence §7
sets out to remove. Antibody–antigen prediction improved markedly over
AlphaFold-Multimer v2.3, though the published gain is ranked over 1,000 model
seeds rather than the usual five, the behaviour §6 reproduces in an open model
and the closing numbers unit collects.

The openness thread also opens on this paper, from the other side. Boltz-1 (§3)
describes itself as the first fully commercially accessible open-source model at
AlphaFold3-level accuracy, and Protenix (§6) states that closed-source models
impede systematic comparison; both lineages exist because AlphaFold3 published
an architecture and the field could not train it. The open reproductions have no
design descendants to trace, so they join their poles here. OpenFold is the
AlQuraishi Lab's retraining of AlphaFold2 on an open reconstruction of its
training set, matching AF2's accuracy, and the source of the roughly
270,000-structure distillation set Boltz-1 trains on. The far end of the
openness axis is OpenFold3: an AF3-class reproduction released under Apache-2.0
with weights, training code and training data, which makes it the one co-folder
in this review that can be retrained from scratch rather than only run — and §8
measures the closed systems against reproducible rather than merely
downloadable. It is a software release rather than a publication: its benchmark
claims are self-reported, and the technical report behind them covers the
superseded preview2 weights, not the current default checkpoint.

That is the reference standing at the start: accuracy on chains and now on
complexes, the scores that the design half grades with, and weights the field
could use but not train. The lineage with a design descendant to follow — and
the one origin here developed independently of AlphaFold — is the next
section's.
